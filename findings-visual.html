Main Finding

Six Practices That Shaped the Experience

When these practices were present, mothers felt held by the system. When absent or inconsistent, mothers found themselves filling the gaps on their own.

1
Proactive Coordinator Engagement
Care team reaches out early — before the mother has time to spiral without guidance.
👩
“I think the worst part of my pregnancy was knowing that something was going on and that I wasn’t in a good place to get that taken care of. When I did finally get to talk to [children’s hospital] and get in with them, it was like a huge weight lifted off my shoulders.” — Participant #61
🕔 The window between suspicion and confirmation was one of the hardest moments ⚡ Early outreach transformed uncertainty into something navigable 🤝 First contact set the tone for how held the mother would feel throughout
2
Coordinated Provider-to-Provider Communication
A dedicated coordinator bridges the care team — so the mother doesn’t have to.
👩
“The case manager is what held it all together.” — Participant #56
👩
“I think the most positive thing and experience was like the level of coordination with the fetal center group.” — Participant #34
🔗 Connected local OB, MFM, and specialty cardiology as one coherent picture 📞 Served as the point of contact between appointments — mothers could reach out with questions 🗂️ Relayed clinical updates so mothers didn’t become the messenger between providers 🗺️ Tracked where the family was in the journey and anticipated what was coming next 💬 Reduced the emotional toll of re-explaining a complex diagnosis at each new encounter
3
Phased, Readiness-Based Information Delivery
Information arrives in emotionally sensitive stages — providers gauge readiness before advancing.
👩
“I really do think things went positive and got more exciting after we had gotten the actual CHD diagnosis and had a game plan. I think I could enjoy my pregnancy again.” — Participant #40
📐 Hand-drawn diagrams and visual tools made complex anatomy concrete without overwhelming 💭 Even excellent delivery couldn’t fully land when grief, fear, and shock were competing for the same mental space ⏱ Pacing across multiple contacts matters — a single session is rarely enough
4
Multidisciplinary Team Involvement
Specialists, social work, and practical guidance come together in an integrated encounter.
👩
“I had a full day with I think maybe like 7 specialists and doctors. I met with a social worker, a genetic counselor. And then we all sat down at a table… I was able to ask more questions just about my son’s outlook and prognosis and what his life might look like.” — Participant #106
🤝 Integration mattered more than volume — seeing the team together was the value 🧩 Bundled visits reduced the sense that care was fragmented across disconnected providers
5
Peer Support and Guided Learning
Families are proactively connected to peer networks — not left to find them on their own.
👩
“You just build a strength that other people don’t understand and a quality that they don’t build… you become a different type of mom.” — Participant #40
💬 Peer communities offered the one space where no explanation was required 🔍 Most mothers found these communities on their own — the care team rarely guided them there ⚠️ Without guidance, online searching could lead to frightening worst-case encounters
6
Birth Preparation
Emotional and practical preparation helps families arrive at delivery with confidence, not shock.
👩
“I learned the importance of advocating for him and I guess in turn, you know, like during the pregnancy, advocate for yourself too.” — Participant #29
📋 Good preparation built self-advocacy, not just information 🏥 NICU environment, separation, and monitoring needed concrete preview
Conceptual Framework

The Two Maternal Pathways

These are not two types of mothers. They are two care contexts that mothers were placed in — often without choice. The same mother, in a different care context, follows a different path.

When the System Absorbs the Burden
  1. Early, proactive outreach — mother is not left waiting in the window between suspicion and care
  2. Information is paced to emotional readiness, not delivered all at once
  3. A coordinator serves as the consistent bridge — mother does not relay between providers
  4. Specialists, social work, and guidance come together in an integrated encounter
  5. Peer support resources are offered proactively — mother is guided, not left to search alone
  6. Preparation before delivery helps the family arrive with confidence rather than shock
  7. Resilience is scaffolded — providers help the mother mark milestones and maintain confidence
Confidence · Preparedness · Sense of being held by the system
When the Mother Fills the Gaps
  1. Late or fragmented referral — local provider limitations leave the mother in limbo
  2. Information is delivered in volume, without pacing — overwhelm precedes understanding
  3. No coordinator, or presence is inconsistent across the care journey
  4. Mother must relay her own history between her local care team and the specialty center
  5. Online searching is unsupported — outcomes vary by what she happens to find
  6. No structured preparation before delivery — some realities of birth and NICU arrive as surprises
  7. Mother must advocate for herself to access what should have been offered proactively
Chronic stress · Unpreparedness · Exhaustion from self-advocacy
Self-advocacy is not a personality trait. It is what happens when the system leaves gaps that the mother must fill herself.
Member Checking · March–April 2026

What Riley’s Care Coordinators Confirmed

Three sessions with Riley/IU Health MFM care coordinators validated and deepened these findings.

🧭
Coordination Is Emotional Triage
Coordinators actively pace information, gauge emotional readiness, and prevent worst-case spirals. When to give information is as important as what to give.
🏥
The Care Conference Is a Major Bundled Intervention
Not a meeting — a full-day structure integrating specialist access, scans, hospital tour, social work, financial guidance, and expectation-setting.
📋
Staged Communication Is the Key Practice
A phased model: early contact → partial orientation at 20 weeks → fuller delivery at 28 weeks → nurse navigator renewal before birth.
🔎
“Don’t Google” Should Become Guided Navigation
The real message: keep track of questions, bring them back to providers, use trusted starting points. Riley currently lacks a branded patient education ecosystem.
💪
Resilience Is Provider-Scaffolded
Coordinators actively help mothers celebrate milestones, normalize reactions, and maintain continuity with preferred providers. Resilience is built together.
🏠
Hospital Preparation Is a Genuine Priority
Families need concrete preparation for what the NICU looks like, how separation works, what monitoring tools are available. Coordinators invest real time here.
Important nuance: Some of what mothers experience as “above and beyond” care at Riley depends on institutional infrastructure — staffing, programmatic support, social work availability, coordinator time. The findings should not attribute benefits only to individual provider goodwill. The system enables the people.
Theoretical Framework

How Mothers Built Resilience

We applied Buzzanell’s Communication Theory of Resilience (CTR) to the data — the first time CTR has been applied to prenatal CHD. All five processes were present.

CTR Process What it looks like in the data
Maintaining Communication NetworksMost prevalent
Using relationships — peers, family, providers — as a buffer and source of support
💻 Online CHD communities (Facebook groups, forums)
🤝 Peer connection with other CHD families
🏠 Extended family networks
Foregrounding Productive Action
Directing energy toward what can be done, rather than what cannot be controlled
📚 Self-education and active research
🗂️ Organizational strategies (schedules, notes, questions)
🧠 Seeking therapy or mental health support
Affirming Identity Anchors
Drawing on stable aspects of self — roles, values, expertise — to stay grounded
🩺 Medical or healthcare background as a coping resource
💪 Claiming a strength or resilience identity
Crafting Normalcy
Creating routines and reframings that make everyday life feel manageable despite disruption
💼 Work as a source of distraction and routine
👪 Prioritizing family time and ordinary moments
🔁 Reframing the experience as something navigable
Developing Alternative Logics
Finding new frameworks for meaning — why this happened, what it means, what comes next
💡 Meaning-making and reframing the diagnosis
🌱 Finding growth or purpose through the experience
🤝 Offering advice and solidarity to future CHD mothers
Key finding: Mothers are not coping alone — they are coping through relationships and communities. Communication networks were the dominant resilience mechanism. When a mother finds her peer community, it doesn’t just feel good. It is how she rebuilds.
A missed opportunity: Mothers sometimes attributed resilience to their baby (“my baby is so strong”) rather than to themselves. Providers can actively affirm maternal resilience in ordinary conversation — something coordinators can do without additional infrastructure.
Research Implications

What This Study Fills

These findings sit at the intersection of two distinct gaps in the prenatal CHD literature.

Practiced Everywhere, Evidence Nowhere
  • Care coordinator presence is nearly universal across major fetal programs — but no formal evidence base exists for its effectiveness
  • Anatomical diagrams for CHD explanation — hand-drawn or electronic, used in nearly every account, completely unstudied
  • Prenatal hospital tours and NICU familiarization — practiced at major centers, no outcome data
  • Peer support programs such as Mended Little Hearts — widely endorsed and valued by mothers, no evaluative literature
Documented Here for the First Time
  • Guided self-research — “Don’t Google CHD” reframed as structured information navigation toward trusted starting points
  • Spouse-shared care coordination — formal division of coordination tasks between partners
  • Work as coping mechanism — for some a stressor, for others a valued outlet and source of identity
  • Visual aids for explaining CHD to extended family — mothers create their own tools; no published literature
  • “Unique wins” reframing — extra ultrasounds and monitoring visits positioned as positive milestones
  • Healthcare professionals as patients — medical self-knowledge as both resource and source of heightened anxiety
